{\large\textbf{Update, week to 28 September}}\\[1pt]
The aim is to read how much of their own enzyme a patient still has, from blood tests taken
over two years while they are on fortnightly infusions. This week: when to take the blood,
which measurements are worth paying for, and a round of checking old claims that cost me five
of them.

\vspace{-7pt}
\hd{When to take the blood}
Each infusion peaks and decays over the fortnight, so when you draw decides what the blood can
tell you. Draws on dosing days all catch the same point of that rise and fall. Spread them and
you see the marker respond to a change whose size and timing you already know, which is what
the enzyme level is read from. Dosing-day draws cost a factor of about \rn{new.alias_cost},
more than anything else in the design.

I had been putting this as ``do not draw on dosing days''. Not enough: every
\rn{new.near_step} days avoids dosing days and still fails, creeping only
\rn{new.near_span_h} hours round the cycle in two years. What matters is covering the cycle.
Each visit sits a little later in it than the last, and they must get the whole way round
before the study ends, which for \rn{new.lap_n} fortnightly visits means at least
\rn{new.lap_eps_h} hours of slip each. A clinic managing only \rn{lnew.n20_n} visits comes
within \rn{week.n20_vs_n25_pct}\,\% of \rn{new.visits} spaced this way.

\hd{Who it works for}
The right-hand plot is the limit, and the uncomfortable part. The estimate is usable
between \rn{week.band_lo_round}\,\% and \rn{new.band_hi_pct}\,\% of normal capacity, and these
patients sit at or below \rn{week.dis_pct}\,\%, roughly \rn{new.disease_factor} times under
it. Two years of monthly draws give \rn{week.sd_nominal} at normal capacity and
\rn{week.sd_disease} at \rn{week.dis_pct}\,\%, against \rn{calc.gate} for a usable answer. So the
precision figures are the rules working in someone healthy, not what this gives in a patient.
That has moved to the top.

Those run in order: the post-infusion sample, then the breath test, then the record. The
tracer sits outside the chain and can be added at any point.

\hd{The dosing schedule}
Changing the therapy does not help enough to justify it. Alternating the dose by
\rn{lnew.alt_amp}\,\% sharpens the answer by \rn{lnew.alt_gain}\,\% and cuts the trough
between doses, the patient's protection, by \rn{lnew.alt_cut}\,\%. Stretching to
\rn{lnew.h28_word} days gives \rn{lnew.h28_gain}\,\% for \rn{lnew.h28_cut}\,\%; \rn{lnew.h7_word} days suits the
patient and costs \rn{lnew.h7_cost}\,\%; continuous infusion suits the patient best and
leaves nothing varying to measure. So optimise the blood schedule and leave the dosing
clinical. If it ever goes continuous, this approach stops working.

\hd{What did not hold up}
\begin{multicols}{2}\raggedcolumns
\begin{itemize}
\item The timescale argument behind a modelling shortcut runs the other way round. The
shortcut holds for a different reason, and only while the patient is steady.
\item One of my checks cannot come out wrong whatever the model is, so it confirms nothing.
It still catches arithmetic slips.
\item A claim about how big a disturbance a patient can take holds only when the carnitine
pool is full. In a depleted patient it reverses, towards calling a dangerous state safe.
\item A factor of \rn{lnew.factor24_old} for how far a threshold moves: no derivation, and
none of the \rn{lnew.pairs_181} endpoint pairs we state reproduces it. Replaced by a window a
clinician can compute per patient.
\item A decision rule for patients in crisis, which tracked another parameter's collapse
rather than the decision.
\end{itemize}
