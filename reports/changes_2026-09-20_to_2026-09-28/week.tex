\section*{The question behind all of it}

In this disease the body cannot break down a substance that food keeps supplying, because
one enzyme is missing or faulty. A new therapy supplies that enzyme by infusion, every two
weeks. \textbf{The question I am working on is whether we can tell, from ordinary blood
tests taken over two years, how much of their own enzyme a patient still has.} That number
would say who needs the therapy most, and how often.

This week I was not trying to produce that number. I was working out what a blood record
has to contain for it to exist at all, which measurements are worth taking, and where the
answer cannot be reached however hard we try. Much of the week went on checking claims I had
already made, and several did not survive.

\section*{1. The clearest result: when to take the blood}

The added enzyme is not steady. It peaks after each infusion and falls about
\rn{week.trough_fold}-fold before the next one, so a patient on this therapy is running
through the same swing every two weeks.

\textbf{That swing is the whole experiment.} If blood is always drawn on dosing days, every
sample catches the patient at the same point of it, and the record is one situation repeated.
Spread the draws through the cycle instead and the record shows the marker responding to a
known change, which is what makes the enzyme level readable.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.82\textwidth]{week_cycle}
\caption{The same number of blood draws, booked two ways. Left: on dosing days, where every
draw sees the same point. Right: each visit booked about half a day later than the last, so
the draws work their way round the cycle.}
\end{figure}

\textbf{The rule that came out of it is a coverage rule.} The visits have to work their way
once round the cycle during the study. Over two years of fortnightly visits, that means
booking them about \rn{week.lap_eps_h_round} hours off the dose interval rather than exactly
on it. Three things make it worth stating carefully:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{The cost of getting it wrong is large.} Drawing blood on dosing days is about
\rn{new.alias_cost} times less precise than spreading the draws out. Nothing else in the
design comes close to that.
\item \textbf{The obvious version of the rule has a hole in it.} ``Do not draw on dosing
days'' sounds sufficient and is not. Booking every \rn{new.near_step} days avoids dosing days
and still fails, because in two years it creeps only \rn{new.near_span_h} hours round the
cycle. A schedule can touch many different points and still cover almost none of the swing.
\item \textbf{It buys back missed visits.} A clinic that can manage twenty visits instead of
twenty-five gets within about \rn{week.n20_vs_n25_pct} per cent of the larger study, simply
by spacing them correctly. Spacing is free; visits are not.
\end{itemize}

\section*{2. Which measurements are worth taking}

I went through every measurement that could go into this study and asked, for each one,
whether it earns its place.

 & \Want

 & \Want
 & The cheapest thing I ask for, and it has to come first. It settles which of two possible

 & \Want

 & \Not

 & \Used

\noindent The three I ask for have to be done in order. The post-infusion sample decides
which model applies; the breath test calibrates it; the long record then estimates the
enzyme level. Skipping to the third is what a study would naturally do, and it would produce
a number nobody could defend.

\section*{3. The limit I had to state plainly}

The method only works when the patient's own enzyme level lies between about
\rn{week.band_lo_round}\,\% and \rn{new.band_hi_pct}\,\% of normal. \textbf{Patients with this
disease sit roughly \rn{new.disease_factor} times below that range.}

So the precision figures in the write-up describe how the rules behave in a healthy person,
not what this study would deliver in a patient. I moved that statement to the front of the
document this week, because a precision quoted before the range it holds over invites exactly
the wrong reading. The design rules themselves hold everywhere. The enzyme estimate does not.

I also checked whether a patient's genetic result could fill the gap. It helps, but it helps
mild patients and hurts severe ones, which is the wrong way round for a disease whose severe
patients are the reason for asking.

\section*{4. Should the therapy be changed to make measuring easier?}

I asked this properly, because it is the obvious idea and it is wrong.

Alternating the dose, or stretching the interval, buys a few per cent of precision
(\rn{lnew.alt_gain}\,\%) at the cost of a much deeper dip between doses
(\rn{lnew.alt_cut}\,\%), which is the patient's protection. Shortening the interval helps the
patient and costs about \rn{lnew.h7_cost}\,\% of precision. A continuous infusion is best of
all for the patient and removes the experiment entirely. There is no middle setting that
suits both.

\textbf{So the recommendation is a division of labour.} The blood schedule is free, so
optimise it. The dosing schedule is paid for out of the patient's safety margin, so leave it
to clinical judgment. The one caveat: if the therapy ever moves to continuous infusion, this
way of measuring stops working, and somebody should notice before rather than after.

\section*{5. What I withdrew}

Five claims did not survive checking. I am listing them because the ones that remain are
worth more for it.

\begin{itemize}
\item A justification I had given for a modelling shortcut ran backwards: I had the two
processes the wrong way round in time. The shortcut is still correct, but for a different
and narrower reason, and it does not hold away from balance.
\item A test I had described as confirming the model cannot fail, whatever the model. It was
arithmetic checking itself. It is still worth running, as a guard against mistakes, but it is
not evidence.
\item A claim about how large a disturbance a patient can absorb held only in the safe
direction. In a depleted patient it reverses, towards calling a dangerous state safe.
\item A figure of \rn{lnew.factor24_old} that I had quoted for how much a threshold moves had
no derivation behind it and could not be reproduced from any of the ranges this work states.
It is replaced by something a clinician can actually compute for a given patient.
\item A decision rule I had proposed for patients in crisis turned out to be reporting a
different quantity's failure rather than the decision. No rule is quoted there now.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Where this leaves things}

The week's output is a shorter list of claims that survive, and a design a clinic could be
handed: take the blood on a schedule that works round the dose cycle, follow one marker
rather than three, run one cheap post-infusion sample before anything else, keep the food
diary honest day by day, and do not expect the enzyme number itself in the patients who need
it most.

Three pieces of analysis are finished but not yet written up: a screen of ten candidate
measurements against what the model can express, a valuation of measuring the enzyme directly
in cells, and a verification status on the one published figure the study's size depends on.
That last matters most, and I would like to settle it next.
