{\large\textbf{Update, week to 28 September}}\\[1pt]
We are trying to work out how much of their own enzyme a patient still has, from blood tests
taken over two years while they are on infusions every fortnight. Most of this week went on
when to take the blood and which measurements are worth paying for. I also went back over
older claims, and five of them did not survive.

\vspace{-7pt}
\hd{When to take the blood}
Each infusion peaks and then decays over the fortnight. Draw on dosing days and every sample
catches the same point of that rise and fall. Spread the draws out and you see the marker move
in response to something whose size and timing you already know, and that response is where
the enzyme level comes from. The difference is a factor of about \rn{new.alias_cost}.

I had been writing this as ``do not draw on dosing days'', which turns out to be too weak.
Every \rn{new.near_step} days avoids dosing days and still fails: over two years it creeps
only \rn{new.near_span_h} hours round the cycle. The visits have to cover the cycle, not just
miss the dose. Each one sits slightly later in it than the last, and they need to get the
whole way round before the study ends, which for \rn{new.lap_n} fortnightly visits works out
at \rn{new.lap_eps_h} hours of slip per visit. One useful side effect: a clinic that can only
manage \rn{lnew.n20_n} visits gets within \rn{week.n20_vs_n25_pct}\,\% of \rn{new.visits}
if it spaces them this way.

\hd{Who it works for}
The right-hand plot is the part I am least happy about. The estimate is usable between
\rn{week.band_lo_round}\,\% and \rn{new.band_hi_pct}\,\% of normal capacity, and these
patients sit at or below \rn{week.dis_pct}\,\%, roughly \rn{new.disease_factor} times under
it. Two years of monthly draws give \rn{week.sd_nominal} at normal capacity and
\rn{week.sd_disease} at \rn{week.dis_pct}\,\%, where we would want \rn{calc.gate}. The
precision figures we quote are really the rules working in a healthy person, and I have moved
that to the top of the document.

They go in order: the post-infusion sample, then the breath test, then the record itself. The
tracer is not part of that chain and can come whenever.

\hd{The dosing schedule}
I checked whether changing the therapy would make measuring easier. It is not worth it.
Alternating the dose by \rn{lnew.alt_amp}\,\% sharpens the answer by \rn{lnew.alt_gain}\,\%
and cuts the trough between doses by \rn{lnew.alt_cut}\,\%, and that trough is what protects
the patient. Stretching to \rn{lnew.h28_word} days gives \rn{lnew.h28_gain}\,\% for
\rn{lnew.h28_cut}\,\%. Cutting to \rn{lnew.h7_word} days is better for the patient and costs
\rn{lnew.h7_cost}\,\%, and continuous infusion is better still but leaves nothing varying to
measure. The blood schedule is the one to optimise; the dosing should stay clinical. Worth
watching: if the therapy ever moves to continuous, this approach stops working.

\hd{What did not hold up}
\begin{multicols}{2}\raggedcolumns
\begin{itemize}
\item The timescale argument I used to justify a modelling shortcut is backwards. The shortcut
is still fine, but for a different reason, and only while the patient is steady.
\item One of my checks cannot come out wrong whatever the model is, so it does not confirm
anything. Still useful for catching arithmetic slips.
\item A claim about how big a disturbance a patient can absorb only holds while the carnitine
pool is full. In a depleted patient it reverses, and calls a dangerous state safe.
\item A factor of \rn{lnew.factor24_old} for how far a threshold moves. No derivation, and
none of the \rn{lnew.pairs_181} endpoint pairs we state gives it. There is a window a
clinician can compute instead.
\item A decision rule for patients in crisis. It was tracking another parameter falling over
rather than the decision itself.
\end{itemize}
