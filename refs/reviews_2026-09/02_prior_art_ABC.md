Prior Work on (A) Periodic Dosing as a Perturbation for Residual-Function Estimation, (B) Sampling-Time Design for Identifiability, and (C) Diet-Input PK/PD Models of Metabolic Disease
TL;DR

* Topic A: No paper explicitly frames periodic therapeutic dosing as a deliberate perturbation for system-identification of an individual's residual organ/enzyme function. The nearest genuine matches are (i) dialysis urea/creatinine kinetic-modeling papers that back-calculate individual residual kidney clearance from the periodic dialysis cycle, and (ii) C-peptide deconvolution papers that recover individual β-cell secretion — both estimate a residual function but frame it as "dialysis adequacy" or "secretion rate," not perturbation-based identification.
* Topic B: A large, mature literature exists — therapeutic-drug-monitoring optimal-sampling, limited-sampling strategies (LSS), and Fisher-information/D-optimal design for multiple-dose/steady-state regimens — but the specific terms "stroboscopic sampling" and "phase-folded sampling" appear only in astronomy/analytical-chemistry, not pharmacology.
* Topic C: Diet-input PK/PD/QSP models exist for PKU (pegvaliase, sapropterin, mechanistic Phe models), for ammonia/urea-cycle metabolism, and translational mRNA-therapy models for MMA/PKU/PA; almost all treat dietary intake as a known/assumed forcing function, and none rigorously treats unrecorded intake as a formally estimated latent/stochastic input.

Key Findings

* The strongest "residual-function-from-periodic-therapy" work is in nephrology (Daugirdas/Depner dialysis kinetics) and endocrinology (C-peptide deconvolution). Both are conceptually adjacent but neither uses the control-theoretic "perturbation/probing-input" framing the query describes — so the exact concept in Topic A appears to be a largely open, novel synthesis.
* Topic B is well covered by named subfields (LSS, TDM optimal sampling, population Fisher-information design), but the astronomy-style vocabulary ("stroboscopic/phase-folded") does not transfer to the pharmacology literature and would be a borrowed term if introduced.
* For Topic C, the pegvaliase PRISM PK/PD model (Harding et al., 2025) and the Moderna translational mRNA model (Baek et al., 2024) are the most relevant modern input–output models; handling of unrecorded diet is uniformly weak — intake is assumed or averaged, not marginalized over.

Details
TOPIC A — Periodic dosing as a perturbation to estimate individual residual organ/enzyme function
Overall verdict: A genuine, explicit match to the exact concept does not appear to exist as a named body of work. The idea — treating a repeatedly/periodically administered therapy as an excitation signal to identify an individual's residual endogenous function — is essentially a novel synthesis. The closest literatures follow.
A1. Nephrology — residual kidney clearance inferred from the periodic dialysis cycle (strongest partial match).

* Daugirdas JT, Depner TA, Greene T, et al. "Standard Kt/Vurea: a method of calculation that includes effects of fluid removal and residual kidney clearance." Kidney International 2010. DOI: 10.1038/ki.2009.525 — Formalizes residual kidney clearance as a parameter within the periodic thrice-weekly dialysis urea cycle; the periodic treatment schedule is the backbone against which residual clearance is quantified.
* Obi Y, Streja E, Daugirdas JT, et al. "Prediction equation for calculating residual kidney urea clearance using urine collections for different hemodialysis treatment frequencies and interdialytic intervals." Nephrology Dialysis Transplantation 2018. DOI: 10.1093/ndt/gfw473 — Derives an individual's residual kidney urea clearance from predialysis urea and urea-reduction ratio across varying dialysis frequencies/interdialytic intervals, using the periodic schedule parameters as inputs.
* Daugirdas JT, Bolasco P. "Creatinine Kinetic Modeling to Estimate Residual Kidney Creatinine Clearance in Patients Being Hemodialyzed Once or Twice Per Week." Seminars in Dialysis 2025. DOI: 10.1111/sdi.70009 — Uses creatinine kinetic modeling across the asymmetric periodic dialysis schedule to back-calculate residual renal creatinine clearance from predialysis serum creatinine without urine collection.
* Shafi T, Michels WM, Levey AS, et al. "Estimating residual kidney function in dialysis patients without urine collection." Kidney International 2016. DOI: 10.1016/j.kint.2015.10.011 — Uses serum filtration markers (β-trace protein, β2-microglobulin, cystatin C) accumulating across the dialysis cycle to estimate individual residual urinary urea clearance (marker-kinetic, not drug-dosing perturbation).

A2. Endocrinology — residual β-cell function via C-peptide deconvolution (strong methodological analog).

* Van Cauter E, Mestrez F, Sturis J, Polonsky KS. "Estimation of insulin secretion rates from C-peptide levels: comparison of individual and standard kinetic parameters for C-peptide clearance." Diabetes 1992;41(3):368–377. DOI: 10.2337/diab.41.3.368 — Reconstructs individual insulin secretion rate by deconvolving plasma C-peptide with a two-compartment model; analyzed 200 decay curves of biosynthetic human C-peptide in normal, obese, and NIDDM subjects to derive standardized clearance parameters, foundational for recovering an individual's residual secretory function.
* Sparacino G, Cobelli C. "A stochastic deconvolution method to reconstruct insulin secretion rate after a glucose stimulus." IEEE Transactions on Biomedical Engineering 1996;43(5):512–529. DOI: 10.1109/10.488799 — Bayesian/regularized deconvolution that recovers the individual insulin secretion rate (an unknown input) from C-peptide after a glucose stimulus, explicitly an input-estimation/system-identification problem robust to nonuniform/infrequent sampling.
* Mortensen SB, Klim S, Dammann B, Kristensen NR, Madsen H, Overgaard RV. "A matlab framework for estimation of NLME models using stochastic differential equations: applications for estimation of insulin secretion rates." Journal of Pharmacokinetics and Pharmacodynamics 2007;34(5):623–642. DOI: 10.1007/s10928-007-9062-4 — Uses SDE-based mixed-effects models to estimate unknown time-varying inputs (insulin secretion rate; time-varying hepatic extraction) from C-peptide/insulin, a direct latent-input identification method.
* Ruan Y, Willemsen RH, Wilinska ME, Tauschmann M, Dunger DB, Hovorka R. "Mixed-meal tolerance test to assess residual beta-cell secretion: beyond the area-under-curve of plasma C-peptide concentration." Pediatric Diabetes 2019;20(3). DOI: 10.1111/pedi.12816 — Uses a validated C-peptide kinetic model during a standardized meal stimulus to estimate individual β-cell responsiveness indices (basal M0, postprandial MI) as measures of residual secretion.

A3. Enzyme replacement therapy / metabolic disease — genuine gap. No paper found uses the periodic dosing schedule of an enzyme therapy to back-calculate an individual's residual endogenous enzyme activity. This is physiologically moot for the leading example, pegvaliase: per StatPearls (NCBI Bookshelf NBK535378), pegvaliase (PEGylated phenylalanine ammonia lyase from Anabaena variabilis) "acts as an enzyme-substitution therapy by degrading phenylalanine independently of PAH activity" — i.e., it converts Phe to ammonia and trans-cinnamic acid regardless of residual PAH, so residual endogenous enzyme is not the estimand. Lysosomal-storage-disease population PK/PD models (systematic review, Clinical Pharmacokinetics 2026, DOI: 10.1007/s40262-026-01636-2) estimate therapeutic-enzyme exposure–response, not residual endogenous activity.
TOPIC B — Sampling-time selection relative to dosing phase for parameter identifiability
B1. Therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) optimal-sampling-time design.

* Dijkman SC, et al. "Individualized Dosing Algorithms and Therapeutic Monitoring for Antiepileptic Drugs." Clinical Pharmacology & Therapeutics 2018. DOI: 10.1002/cpt.777 — Shows via simulation across 11 antiepileptic drugs that individual clearance estimated from TDM samples at chosen times relative to the dose personalizes dosing; explicitly notes trough is the usual but not always the optimal sampling time (some drugs favor 2–6 h post-dose).
* Best-practice TDM reviews (e.g., British Journal of Clinical Pharmacology, PMC1873661) establish that samples must be timed relative to dose and steady state (trough vs peak) for valid parameter interpretation — foundational but not identifiability-theoretic.

B2. Limited sampling strategies (LSS) for AUC/PK-parameter estimation.

* (Carboplatin) "A limited sampling strategy for determining carboplatin AUC and monitoring drug dosage." Cancer Chemother Pharmacol (PubMed 10741287) — Bayesian NONMEM-based two-sample schedule (1 and 4 h post-infusion) estimates carboplatin clearance/AUC with non-significant bias (−4.5%).
* "A Method for Evaluating Robustness of Limited Sampling Strategies — Exemplified by Serum Iohexol Clearance for Determination of Measured GFR." Pharmaceutics 2023 (PMC10143161) — Shows a 4-point iohexol LSS for GFR is robust to deviations from optimal sampling times (P15 rising only from 5.3% to a maximum 8.3% under random timing error).
* Disease-specific LSS papers (mycophenolic acid in renal/liver/lung transplant; clozapine; polymyxin B; anti-TB drugs) develop reduced blood-draw sets timed within the dosing interval to estimate AUC — a broad, heavily populated area.

B3. "Stroboscopic sampling" / "phase-folded sampling." These exact terms do not appear in the pharmacology/chronopharmacology literature. "Phase folding" and "stroboscopic" period-resolved sampling are established in astronomy (period-finding in variable-star/pulsar light curves); separately, "stroboscopic sampling" is used in two-dimensional analytical chemistry (HPLC–capillary-electrophoresis interfacing, PubMed 15095438). No pharmacological usage of either term was found — introducing it into pharmacometrics would be a deliberate borrowing.
B4. Optimal design theory (D-optimal / Fisher-information) for multiple-dose/steady-state PK/PD sampling.

* Aarons L, Ogungbenro K. "Optimal Design of Pharmacokinetic Studies." Basic & Clinical Pharmacology & Toxicology 2010. DOI: 10.1111/j.1742-7843.2009.00533.x — Reviews Fisher-information-based optimal sampling design including steady-state and multiple-dose regimens and a drug–drug-interaction case (midazolam given 2 h after an inhibitor at steady state), directly on point for phase-relative sampling.
* Ogungbenro K, Aarons L, et al. "Optimal Design for Multivariate Response Pharmacokinetic Models." Journal of Pharmacokinetics and Pharmacodynamics 2007. DOI: 10.1007/s10928-006-9009-1 — Develops D-optimal multi-response sampling-time design maximizing the Fisher information determinant for parent/metabolite and simultaneous PK/PD measurements.
* Duffull SB, et al. "Simultaneous optimal experimental design on dose and sample times." Journal of Pharmacokinetics and Pharmacodynamics 2009. DOI: 10.1007/s10928-009-9114-z — Jointly optimizes dose and sampling times via the Fisher information matrix for PK/PD parameter precision.
* Foundational methods: D'Argenio DZ, "Incorporating prior parameter uncertainty in the design of sampling schedules…" Math Biosci 1990; the levofloxacin population optimal-sampling paper (Drusano/Preston et al., Antimicrob Agents Chemother 2003, aac.asm.org/content/47/9/2888) applies D-optimality (via ADAPT II) to steady-state/population schedules; and Tod/Rocchisani metacycline work on ED/EID/API criteria.

TOPIC C — PK/PD/QSP models of metabolic-disease biomarkers with dietary intake as a model input
C1. PKU — pegvaliase (Palynziq).

* Harding CO, Bulloch Whitehall K, Lindstrom K, Lilienstein J, Sazova O, Levy DG. "Long-term management strategies for pegvaliase use in phenylketonuria: Lessons learned from the phase-3 PRISM open-label extension study." Genetics in Medicine 2025 (PII S1098-3600(25)00106-6; verify DOI at publisher). — Develops an individual-level PK/PD model relating pegvaliase clearance (declining with immune tolerization), dose, and dietary Phe intake to blood Phe; dietary Phe enters as a model input, and the model shows blood Phe and diet become uncoupled as clearance falls (with a high-sensitivity/volatility window at ~120–200 μmol/L Phe). The anchoring PRISM efficacy data (Thomas J, et al., Mol Genet Metab 2018, PII S1096719218300210): of 261 participants, within 24 months 68.4% achieved blood Phe ≤600 μmol/L, 60.7% ≤360 μmol/L, and 51.2% ≤120 μmol/L, with mean blood Phe falling from 1232.7 (SD 386.4) μmol/L at baseline to 311.4 (427) at 24 months.
* Qi Y, Patel G, Henshaw J, et al. "Pharmacokinetic, pharmacodynamic, and immunogenic rationale for optimal dosing of pegvaliase." Clinical and Translational Science 2021. DOI: 10.1111/cts.13043 — Characterizes steady-state pegvaliase PK (flat 24-h profile, terminal half-life ~47–60 h) and blood-Phe PD across induction/titration/maintenance, but treats diet as background rather than a formally modeled stochastic input.
* Handling of unrecorded intake: diet is trial-recorded, not stochastic. The PRISM design documents this limitation directly — >80% of patients were not adhering to medical nutrition therapy at baseline and consumed substantial natural protein (mean ~39 g/day), i.e., intake was measured/estimated as a covariate, not marginalized over as a latent variable.

C2. PKU — sapropterin (Kuvan/BH4) and mechanistic Phe models.

* Feillet F, et al. "Pharmacokinetics of Sapropterin in Patients with Phenylketonuria." Clinical Pharmacokinetics 2008. DOI: 10.2165/0003088-200847120-00006 — Population PK of BH4 (apparent clearance, volume, absorption rate with IIV); PD (blood Phe) and diet are not modeled as a driving input.
* SPARK trial population PK (sapropterin in children <4 y). Orphanet Journal of Rare Diseases 2017 (PMC5343543) — One-compartment flip-flop popPK favoring once-daily dosing with weight adjustment; measures Phe tolerance but does not model diet as a forcing function.
* Kaufman S. "A model of human phenylalanine metabolism in normal subjects and in phenylketonuric patients." PNAS 1999;96(6):3160. DOI: 10.1073/pnas.96.6.3160 — Mechanistic kinetic model (two-site ordered-binding PAH kinetics) with dietary Phe intake as input; explicitly designed to estimate residual PAH activity from the clearance of a Phe load, assuming a specified intake.
* Langenbeck U, et al. "Modelling the phenylalanine blood level response during treatment of phenylketonuria." Journal of Inherited Metabolic Disease 2001. DOI: 10.1023/A:1013946006155 — Single-compartment model with alimentary Phe input and two outputs (first-order Phe→Tyr conversion plus zero-order Phe use for net protein synthesis), applied to routine monitoring data; assumes intake is known.
* Handling of unrecorded intake: these mechanistic PKU models generally assume a known/idealized intake or steady state; none formally marginalize over intake uncertainty.

C3. Methylmalonic acidemia (MMA).

* Baek R, Coughlan K, Jiang L, et al. (Moderna, Inc.). "Characterizing the mechanism of action for mRNA therapeutics for the treatment of propionic acidemia, methylmalonic acidemia, and phenylketonuria." Nature Communications 2024;15(1):3804. DOI: 10.1038/s41467-024-47460-9 — Semi-mechanistic translational PK/PD models linking mRNA → protein/activity → biomarkers (plasma MMA, 2-MC, C3/C2 ratio) in murine models, scaled allometrically to predict first-in-human starting doses of 0.3 mg/kg q3w (mRNA-3927, PA), 0.1 mg/kg q3w (mRNA-3705, MMA), and 0.4 mg/kg/week (mRNA-3210, PKU); diet is not an explicit modeled input.
* An J, et al. "Systemic Messenger RNA Therapy as a Treatment for Methylmalonic Acidemia." Cell Reports 2017 (DOI: 10.1016/j.celrep.2017.11.049) — Preclinical hMUT mRNA PK/PD in mouse models (75–85% plasma MMA reduction); biomarker dynamics modeled, diet not an input.
* No human semi-mechanistic PK/PD model of MMA was found that includes dietary/natural protein intake as an explicit dynamic driving forcing function with formal handling of intake uncertainty. In the human MMA literature dietary protein appears only as a correlate in cross-sectional diet–biomarker analyses (e.g., Genetics in Medicine 2015, gim2015102; Am J Clin Nutr variable-dietary-management study), not as a model input.

C4. Urea cycle disorders (UCD).

* Griffin JWD, Bradshaw PC. "Effects of a high protein diet and liver disease in an in silico model of human ammonia metabolism." Theoretical Biology and Medical Modelling 2019;16:11. DOI: 10.1186/s12976-019-0109-1 — ODE model (blood ammonia/urea/glutamine) with dietary protein intake and enzyme (CPS1) activity as explicit inputs; increasing dietary protein by 72% raised blood ammonia by 59%, and simulated heterozygous CPS1 loss-of-function more than tripled blood ammonia (~18→60 μM depending on protein intake). Protein enters as CPS1-adjusted rate inputs (e.g., 71/100/122 g/day mapped to defined reaction velocities).
* "Blood ammonia and glutamine as predictors of hyperammonemic crises in patients with urea cycle disorder." Genetics in Medicine 2015. DOI: 10.1038/gim.2014.148 — Relates fasting and 24-h AUC ammonia to crisis risk with dietary protein intake as a covariate; a statistical exposure–risk model rather than a dynamic diet-input PK/PD model.
* Handling of unrecorded intake: the in silico UCD model assumes fixed protein-intake levels (perfect intake, continuous nutritional supply); no stochastic/latent-input treatment was found.

C5. Cross-cutting methodology for unmeasured/uncertain dietary input. No PKU/MMA/UCD paper was found that formally treats unrecorded diet as a latent stochastic input. The methods that could do this exist in the general pharmacometrics literature — SDE-based NLME "grey-box" models that estimate unknown time-varying inputs (Mortensen et al. 2007, DOI: 10.1007/s10928-007-9062-4; Tornøe et al. grey-box PK/PD, J Pharmacokinet Pharmacodyn 2004) and nonparametric input/deconvolution estimation (Sparacino & Cobelli, DOI: 10.1109/10.488799). To date these have been applied to insulin secretion, not to dietary Phe/protein intake.
Recommendations

* If the goal is to establish novelty for Topic A: the perturbation/system-identification framing applied to periodic therapeutic dosing for individual residual-function estimation appears open. Position the work against the dialysis-kinetics (Daugirdas/Depner/Obi) and C-peptide-deconvolution (Van Cauter; Sparacino & Cobelli; Mortensen) precedents, which are the true prior art; explicitly note that these estimate residual clearance/secretion but do not adopt the control-theoretic "probing input" framing.
* For Topic B: adopt the established D-optimal/Fisher-information machinery (Ogungbenro & Aarons; Duffull; Aarons & Ogungbenro) and LSS methodology directly. If you introduce "stroboscopic/phase-folded sampling" language into pharmacology, flag it as a term borrowed from astronomy, since it has no pharmacological precedent — this is a defensible naming contribution rather than a re-derivation.
* For Topic C: the clear methodological gap is formal handling of unrecorded diet. Combine a mechanistic Phe/ammonia/MMA model (Kaufman; Langenbeck; Griffin & Bradshaw; Baek et al.) with SDE-NLME latent-input estimation (Mortensen et al.) or Bayesian/nonparametric deconvolution to marginalize over intake uncertainty — this is a defensible, apparently unoccupied niche, most tractable in PKU where blood Phe is densely monitored.
* Thresholds that would change these conclusions: discovery of a nephrology or pharmacometrics paper that explicitly uses "system identification"/"probing input" language with periodic dosing (would weaken Topic A novelty); or a PKU/MMA/UCD model paper that estimates diet as a stochastic latent state (would fill the Topic C gap).

Caveats

* Several DOIs recovered from secondary indexing should be verified against the publisher record before formal citation — specifically the Harding 2025 Genetics in Medicine DOI (only the Elsevier PII S1098-3600(25)00106-6 was confirmed), the Obi/Daugirdas 2018 NDT article number, and the exact volume/DOI of the 2021 pegvaliase Clinical and Translational Science paper (PMID 34057292 confirmed).
* "Nothing found" statements reflect thorough but not exhaustive searching (~18 web searches plus a targeted deep-dive). Absence of an exact-match paper is a stronger claim for the unusual terms ("stroboscopic sampling" in pharmacology; "periodic dosing as perturbation for residual enzyme ID") than for the broad, heavily populated LSS/optimal-design areas, where additional disease-specific instances certainly exist.
* Some retrieved items are preprints, patents, or reviews rather than primary peer-reviewed articles; these are labeled as such in-text and should be weighted accordingly. The Topic A "gap" conclusions are analytic judgments about framing, not proof of absence — a paper using different terminology could exist.
