\begin{abstract}
\noindent
This note states a control-theoretic problem arising in the individualised dosing of
an mRNA enzyme-replacement therapy for a monogenic metabolic disease. The clinical
decision is the choice of a dosing interval. The quantity that determines it is the
patient's residual enzymatic capacity, which is never measured. We formulate three
candidate models, alternative hypotheses about the non-enzymatic clearance term rather
than stages of a development, and state explicitly what is known, measured and unknown
in each. In the operating regime in which patients are clinically maintained, the two
clearance terms separate in principle and not in practice. Over the range a patient's
marker visits, total clearance is affine to within assay error, and the residual capacity
enters only through the intercept of that fit, which the range can reach only by
extrapolation and which carries the half-saturation constant with it. The decision the
capacity is wanted for survives that: the safety margin is linear in the direction the data
cannot resolve where the capacity is quadratic in it, so the sign of the margin is
determined despite the capacity being unknown to two orders of magnitude, except for
illness amplitudes inside a computable window. A perturbation acting selectively on one
channel renders the system formally identifiable, and we show that the determinant of the
resulting problem depends on the metabolite levels before and after the perturbation and
on the half-saturation constant, and not on which channel was perturbed. The analysis is therefore carried out on
carnitine, which is administered to every accessible patient, and transfers unchanged to
the therapy, which is administered to none. The resulting problem is severely
ill-conditioned in either case. We quantify this and list the questions that remain open.
\end{abstract}
