\begin{abstract}
\noindent
The clinical decision in mRNA enzyme replacement for propionic acidemia is a dosing
interval, and it is set by a quantity nobody measures: the patient's residual enzymatic
capacity $\theta$.

The trial is already running the experiment that identifies it. The therapy is a
known, exogenous input acting selectively on $\theta$, and it supplies forty-two times
the excitation the marker's own excursions do.
Monthly outpatient draws across dose cycles identify $\theta$ to $17\%$ over two years,
and thirteen two-monthly draws to $19\%$, given the non-enzymatic clearance within a
factor of three and the delivered increment characterised to about $12\%$. Nothing is
assumed about $K_m$. One scheduling choice destroys it, and it is the obvious one:
sampling on dosing days, or at any multiple of the dose interval, takes the precision from
$0.17$ to $3.51$, and moving the schedule half a day recovers it.

Two limits bound it. The dose train is the design's condition of existence rather than an
improvement on it: nothing else supplies enough excitation, spontaneous load variability
alone gives $1.79$, and the design therefore reaches only patients on the therapy. And
across a heterogeneous cohort the median is $0.19$, with just over half of patients meeting
a $20\%$ target, because the dose is fixed in absolute terms while capacity is not.

Identification is not the binding constraint on the decision: the threshold $\theta$ would
be compared against is stipulated rather than measured, and moves by a factor of $24$
across its defensible range. The note therefore ends in a data specification rather than a
dosing rule, and its first tier is one unpublished measurement, the same-day repeatability
of a $1$-$^{13}$C-propionate breath test across a single dose.
\end{abstract}
